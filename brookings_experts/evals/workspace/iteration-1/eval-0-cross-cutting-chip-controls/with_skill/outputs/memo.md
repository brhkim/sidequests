# Brookings experts on the new chip export controls and the allied-alignment fight

**Topic:** A fresh round of U.S. semiconductor export restrictions, and the friction it is creating with the allied governments (Netherlands, Japan, Korea, the EU) whose equipment makers absorb much of the cost. The live question is less "are controls good" than "can Washington still get allies to march in step, and what does Beijing do about it."
**Read as:** Media comment / background briefing — "background call tomorrow afternoon" and "consult" signal a reporter or analyst who wants an on-the-record-adjacent conversation on a fast-moving story. Built for recency first; it degrades gracefully if you actually want a formal briefing.
**Roster snapshot:** 2026-09-13 build · **Checked:** 2026-09-13

A note before the list: I could not pin down from open search the specific rule announced today. The verifiable recent thread is the Sept. 2025 affiliates rule (enforcement paused into late 2026), the December 2025 decision to license Nvidia H200-class exports, and legislative pushes — the MATCH Act, the STRIDE Act — to force allied alignment on semiconductor manufacturing equipment. Every scholar below is placed against that thread, so confirm the specific rule with them at the top of the call.

## Recommendations

### 1. Kyle Chan — Fellow, Foreign Policy, John L. Thornton China Center

**Why them.** Chan is the one person on this roster whose central subject is exactly the mechanism at issue: Chinese industrial policy in semiconductors and what controls actually do to it. He is not a generalist China hand with a chip file — he tracks CXMT, SMIC, domestic toolmaking and the compute constraint on Chinese AI labs at component level, which is what you need if the story turns on whether the new rule bites or just accelerates localization. He is also the rare voice who says both things at once: controls *have* slowed Chinese labs, and U.S. chipmakers now hold roughly zero share of the China AI market. On the ally question he is the supply-chain-topology person — he can tell you which chokepoints the Dutch and Japanese actually hold and which are already eroding.

**Recent work.**
- [Testimony before the House Select Committee on the CCP, "China's Campaign to Steal America's AI Edge"](https://docs.house.gov/meetings/ZS/ZS00/20260416/119165/HHRG-119-ZS00-Wstate-ChanK-20260416.pdf) — April 16, 2026
- [Competing AI strategies for the US and China](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/competing-ai-strategies-for-the-us-and-china/) — June 10, 2026
- [Are the US and China really in an AI "race"?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/are-the-us-and-china-really-in-an-ai-race/) — July 2026 (Beijing Brief podcast, with R. David Edelman)
- [What happened when Trump met Xi?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-happened-when-trump-met-xi/) — November 5, 2025 (his section is on rare earths as counter-leverage)

**Background.** Fellow in the Thornton China Center working on China's industrial policy across AI, robotics, EVs, batteries and semiconductors. He is tagged across global trade, U.S. trade policy, manufacturing and supply chains, geopolitics and technology — the only name in the index sitting on all of those at once, which is a fair proxy for how cross-cutting his beat is.

**Access.** Resident, mid-career fellow — no named chair, no program to run. This is the profile that answers email on deadline. He testifies, podcasts and gives broadcast interviews, so he is comfortable on background and unlikely to need a week of comms handling. Route through Brookings Foreign Policy media relations, but expect a fast turn.

### 2. Mireya Solís — Director, Center for Asia Policy Studies; Philip Knight Chair in Japan Studies

**Why them.** She is the allied-grumbling half of your story, and nobody else on this list is. Solís works on Japanese foreign economic policy and U.S. economic statecraft in Asia — the discipline of *how allies experience being conscripted into someone else's export-control regime*. Japan is the second-largest holder of semiconductor equipment chokepoints after the Netherlands, and Tokyo's stated position has been to assess the impact of existing curbs before accepting more. Solís has been running Brookings' programming on exactly that: economic security, chokepoints, and Japan's strategic reset under Takaichi. If your call needs someone who can articulate the allied objection rather than characterize it, this is the call.

**Recent work.**
- [Economic security and mega chokepoints: Japan's strategic reset](https://www.brookings.edu/events/economic-security-and-mega-chokepoints-japans-strategic-reset/) — May 4, 2026 (she convened and moderated; panel included Japanese economic-security officials)
- [New frontiers in the trade-economic security nexus in Asia](https://www.brookings.edu/events/new-frontiers-in-the-trade-economic-security-nexus-in-asia/) — May 15, 2026
- [Late-breaking shocks and shifting goalposts: Takaichi's highwire Washington visit](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/late-breaking-shocks-and-shifting-goalposts-takaichis-highwire-washington-visit/) — February 23, 2026 (with Eiki Tagami)

**Background.** Author of *Dilemmas of a Trading Nation*; her work is squarely on the trade/economic-security nexus rather than on chips as a technology. Expect strong framing on allied political economy and less on the technical scope of the rule.

**Access.** Program director with a named chair — the hardest calendar on this list, and booking goes through Brookings comms. Worth the wait if the ally angle is the spine of your piece; if you need someone by tomorrow afternoon, ask her office whether a Center for Asia Policy Studies colleague can cover, or lead with Chan and add her later. Caveat on fit: her recent output is Japan-centric, so she is thinner on the Dutch/ASML side.

### 3. Mark MacCarthy — Nonresident Senior Fellow, Governance Studies, Center for Technology Innovation

**Why them.** He is the most prolific Brookings writer on chip export controls as a policy question, and he is on the skeptical side of it — which is useful on a story where most sourcing will come from people who designed the controls. His argument is specific and falsifiable: if superintelligence is not imminent, then the marginal control mostly cedes the world's largest chip market to Huawei without buying security. He has written it twice in 2026, before and after the H200 decision, so he can tell you what the Trump administration's loosening was reasoning from and where the new tightening cuts against it. On a story where "allies are grumbling," he supplies the argument the allies are privately making about commercial cost.

**Recent work.**
- [Ball game's over — the US is out of the AI chip market in China](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/ball-games-over-the-us-is-out-of-the-ai-chip-market-in-china/) — June 17, 2026
- [If superintelligence isn't imminent, the Trump administration may be right to loosen advanced chip export controls](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/if-superintelligence-isnt-imminent-the-trump-administration-may-be-right-to-loosen-advanced-chip-export-controls/) — February 26, 2026

**Background.** Longtime technology policy hand; his Brookings work sits in the Center for Technology Innovation and the Section 230 Project, and his roster topics are internet, regulatory policy and media rather than China or trade. That is the honest limit: he is a tech-regulation analyst arguing about chip controls, not a China specialist. Verify any China-internal claim with Chan.

**Access.** Nonresident senior fellow — Brookings is his side engagement, so replies can be slower and his primary affiliation may route press separately. He publishes frequently and takes clear positions, which usually correlates with willingness to talk.

### 4. Daniel S. Hamilton — Nonresident Senior Fellow, Foreign Policy, Center on the United States and Europe

**Why them.** The European end of "allies grumbling" is a different fight from the Asian one: it runs through Dutch equipment exports, EU economic-security instruments, and Beijing's standing demand that Brussels drop its chipmaking-equipment restrictions. Hamilton has been writing that seam continuously, including four days ago. He argues the dependency runs both ways — that China is roughly twice as dependent on the EU for strategic imports as the reverse — which is a concrete, quotable counter to the "Europe has no leverage" framing your other sources will offer.

**Recent work.**
- [Europe's China Shock 2.0: Where does it go from here?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/europes-china-shock-2-0-where-does-it-go-from-here/) — September 9, 2026
- [How should Europe position itself for systemic rivalry with China?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/how-should-europe-position-itself-for-systemic-rivalry-with-china/) — December 5, 2025
- [Between Washington and Beijing: How Europe fits into US-China strategic competition](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/between-washington-and-beijing-how-europe-fits-into-us-china-strategic-competition/) — September 11, 2025 (with Czin, O'Hanlon, Thornton, Varma and Wright; includes the Netherlands-semiconductors thread)

**Background.** Transatlantic economic relations specialist; his Brookings topics are global trade, defense and security, and his regions are Europe, Germany and Russia. He is a Europe hand, not a semiconductor one — ask him about Brussels' calculus, not about lithography.

**Access.** Nonresident senior fellow with an outside academic base, so his own institution may handle press. Historically a frequent commentator and part of Brookings' rapid-response roundups, which suggests he is reachable on short notice.

### 5. Jonathan A. Czin — Michael H. Armacost Chair in Foreign Policy Studies; Fellow, John L. Thornton China Center

**Why them.** Czin covers the angle nobody else here does: what Beijing thinks it is buying. He argued after the November 2025 Trump-Xi meeting that suspending the affiliates rule "set a pernicious precedent of trading away U.S. export controls, which had previously been non-negotiable" — which is precisely the frame for a new tightening that lands while a truce is nominally in effect. If your story has a "does this stick, or is it a bargaining chip" question, he is the source.

**Recent work.**
- [Europe's China Shock 2.0: Where does it go from here?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/europes-china-shock-2-0-where-does-it-go-from-here/) — September 9, 2026 (contributor)
- [What happened when Trump met Xi?](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-happened-when-trump-met-xi/) — November 5, 2025 (the export-controls-as-precedent argument)
- [What Beijing wants from a US-China trade war](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/what-beijing-wants-from-a-us-china-trade-war/) — March 12, 2025

**Background.** Former director for China at the White House National Security Council (2021-2023) and previously a senior China analyst at the CIA. That pedigree is why he is worth the call on intent and negotiation dynamics; it also means he will be careful about what he characterizes.

**Access.** Resident fellow with a named chair — mid-career seniority, so more available than a program director, and he appears regularly in Brookings' fast-turn expert roundups. Good bet for tomorrow.

## Why this order

Chan is first because the ranking is decided by whose current, dated work is on the precise question: he testified on it in April, published on it in June, and podcast on it in July, and his subject *is* the chip supply chain rather than the diplomacy around it. Solís is second not on volume but because your story has two halves and she owns the half Chan does not — but you are trading access for that, since her calendar is the worst on this list. MacCarthy is third because he writes about chip controls more often than anyone else here and takes a contrary position, which is valuable, but he is a technology-regulation analyst rather than a China or trade specialist and you should not lean on him for facts about China's domestic capacity. Hamilton and Czin are fourth and fifth because each is excellent on one dimension — Europe, and Beijing's read — while being adjacent to the chips themselves. Going down the list trades subject-matter precision for diplomatic context; if you can only make one call, make it Chan and ask him for the allied read as a second question.

## Gaps

- **No Dutch or ASML-specific expert.** The Netherlands is the single most important allied chokepoint and Brookings has no scholar filed under it. Hamilton is the closest, at the EU-policy level of abstraction. For the lithography specifics you will need CSIS, Bruegel or a Dutch institute.
- **No export-control lawyer or BIS practitioner.** Nobody on this roster does licensing mechanics, the foreign direct product rule, or enforcement. If the story turns on what the rule's text actually reaches, that source is not at Brookings.
- **Korea is thin and Taiwan is unaddressed.** TSMC's position is central to any chip-controls story; Brookings' Taiwan expertise (Hass, Richard Bush, Patricia Kim) is security- and cross-strait-focused rather than industrial.
- **Today's specific rule is unverified.** I could not confirm from open search which measure was announced today, so every fit judgment above is against the 2025-26 policy arc, not against the text of the new rule.
