# Who to call at Brookings on semiconductor export controls

**For:** background call, tomorrow afternoon
**Date:** September 13, 2026

## The short answer

Your story has two halves — (1) the new controls themselves and whether they
work, and (2) allies grumbling about being squeezed — and **no single Brookings
person owns both.** Brookings' bench here is genuinely thinner than CSIS's or
CSET's on export-control mechanics, but it is strong on two things those shops
are weaker on: the industry's real economics, and how Tokyo, Seoul and The Hague
actually think about being pulled along.

So: pick one from each column, or pick Chris Thomas if you only get one call.

---

## First call: Christopher A. Thomas

**Nonresident Senior Fellow, Foreign Policy, Brookings.** Also chairman of
Integrated Insights and a visiting professor at Tsinghua.

**Why him:** He is the only person on this list who has actually run the
business. Ten years at Intel, including as General Manager of Intel China, then
ten years at McKinsey where he led the Asia Semiconductor Practice. He will tell
you what a given rule does to a specific tool, a specific fab, a specific
supplier's order book — which is exactly the layer where "allies are being
squeezed" stops being a talking point and becomes Tokyo Electron's revenue.

**What he has argued:** In *Lagging but motivated: The state of China's
semiconductor industry* he made the case that full Chinese technology
independence is economically unrealistic, but that entity-listing and export
controls push Chinese firms toward indigenization as a *risk-management* choice
by business leaders, not just a state mandate. That framing has aged well and is
the sharpest available counter to a simple "controls are working / controls
backfired" binary.

**Caveats:** His signature Brookings work (*A Semiconductor Strategy for the
United States*, 2022; the China industry piece, 2021) predates the current rule
set. He is nonresident and based partly in Asia — check availability early, and
confirm with Brookings comms whether he is currently speaking on the record
given his corporate board roles.

---

## Second call, on the allied-friction half: Mireya Solís

**Director, Center for Asia Policy Studies; Senior Fellow, Foreign Policy;
Philip Knight Chair in Japan Studies.**

**Why her:** She is the best-positioned Brookings expert on precisely the
grievance in your story. Her work on American economic statecraft in Asia argues
that Washington's partners support the *goal* of restricting China's access but
diverge sharply on *implementation* — Japan's instinct is to protect and nurture
its own technologies rather than to slow China down, and Seoul restricts
narrowly because roughly 60% of its semiconductor exports go to China. She has
also made the point that allies often lacked the domestic legal authority to
mirror a US country-specific rule even when willing, which is the unglamorous
reason a lot of "alignment" fails.

**Useful specifics she can source:** the 2023 piece with Seong-ho Sheen on how
South Korea sees tech competition and export controls; her ongoing work on
Japanese economic security and Chinese economic coercion (March 2026).

**Caveat:** She is a political economist on Japan and Asian trade architecture,
not an export-control lawyer. Do not ask her to parse Foreign Direct Product
Rule mechanics.

---

## If you want the China-capability read: Kyle Chan

**Fellow, John L. Thornton China Center.**

**Why him:** He is Brookings' most active current voice on Chinese industrial
policy across AI, chips, batteries and EVs, he writes the *High Capacity*
newsletter, and he testified before the House Select Committee on the CCP in
April 2026 ("China's Campaign to Steal America's AI Edge"). He is the person to
call for hard numbers on Huawei Ascend and Cambricon production volumes and on
how much domestic substitution has actually materialized — the empirical test of
whether the new rules bite.

**Caveat:** His lane is China's build-out, not allied diplomacy or US
rulemaking.

---

## If your angle is "the controls are counterproductive": Mark MacCarthy

**Nonresident Senior Fellow, Governance Studies and the Center for Technology
Innovation.**

**Why him:** He is the most prolific and most recent Brookings writer on this
exact debate, and he has a clear, quotable thesis. February 2026: if
superintelligence is not imminent, the premise behind the strict 2022–2025
controls collapses, and loosening is defensible. June 2026: *Ball game's over —
the US is out of the AI chip market in China*, arguing that even after the H200
approval Beijing blocked purchases, and that US chipmakers have lost that market
permanently.

**Caveats, and they matter:** MacCarthy is a tech-governance and platform-
regulation scholar by training, not a semiconductor or China specialist. He is
also a strong advocate on one side of this argument — which makes him a great
on-the-record voice and a poor neutral explainer. If you use him, balance him.

## Also on the roster, lower priority

- **Sarah Kreps** — Nonresident Senior Fellow, Foreign Policy / Strobe Talbott
  Center; director of Cornell's Tech Policy Lab. Co-authored an early (2022)
  argument against full reshoring in favor of deepening allied partnerships and
  fixing high-skilled immigration. Good on supply-chain strategy writ large;
  dated on the current rules.
- **John Villasenor** — Nonresident Senior Fellow, CTI. Wrote the January 2025
  critique of the AI diffusion rule's three-tier country structure, arguing it
  pushes middle-tier countries toward non-US compute. Relevant if the new rules
  revive tiering.
- **Ryan Hass** — Director, John L. Thornton China Center. Not an export-control
  specialist, but the right call if you need the strategic-relationship frame
  rather than the technology.

## Two people to cross off

- **Joshua P. Meltzer** (trade, Global Economy and Development) — listed as a
  **former** Brookings expert. Do not pitch the call to Brookings comms.
- **Carrick Flynn / Saif Khan** — their well-known EUV and "keeping China
  dependent on democracies" pieces were *published by* Brookings in 2020 but
  they were CSET (Georgetown) researchers, not Brookings fellows. Easy citation
  trap.

## One honest flag

On the narrow question of what this week's rules actually say and how allied
legal authority in Japan and the Netherlands does or doesn't permit matching
them, the deepest published work is at CSIS, not Brookings. If your editor wants
a technical explainer rather than a strategic read, Brookings may not be the
right building — but Thomas plus Solís will give you something CSIS won't: the
commercial reality underneath the rule, and why the grumbling from Tokyo and The
Hague is about domestic law and revenue exposure rather than about principle.

## Suggested questions

1. Which specific tools and suppliers does this rule actually reach, and whose
   order books does it hit hardest? (Thomas)
2. Do Japan and the Netherlands have the domestic legal authority to match a
   US country-specific rule, or are they being asked for something their
   statutes can't deliver? (Solís)
3. What has domestic Chinese substitution actually produced this year, in
   units? (Chan)
4. Is there any evidence controls have slowed Chinese capability, as opposed to
   redirecting where it's built? (Thomas, then MacCarthy for the sharp version)
