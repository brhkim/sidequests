# Brookings experts on post-pandemic chronic absenteeism and what districts are doing about it

**Topic:** Why chronic absenteeism has stayed roughly double its pre-pandemic level, and whether the interventions districts have actually deployed — attendance campaigns, incentives, home visits, engagement redesign, staffing fixes — are working.
**Read as:** Speaking / convening — "panel," "convening in the spring," "good on stage." Ranked for stage presence and range as well as evidence of current work; a media-comment list would look somewhat different (noted at the end).
**Roster snapshot:** 2026-09-13 · **Checked:** 2026-09-13

One finding shapes this whole memo, so it belongs up front. Brookings is a major *publisher* on chronic absenteeism but a thin *owner* of it. The three most-cited absenteeism pieces on brookings.edu are all by outside guest authors — UNC's EPIC team, a USC survey team, and a 2018 Hamilton Project piece — and Brookings' own January 2025 convening on the subject filled its panel with an AEI fellow, a sitting superintendent, and a nonprofit chief learning officer. Only two Brookings staff scholars have current work of their own that touches absenteeism directly. That is a real constraint on a panel, and the Gaps section says what to do about it.

## Recommendations

### 1. Rebecca Winthrop — Director, Center for Universal Education; Senior Fellow, Global Economy and Development

**Why them.** Winthrop is the one Brookings scholar with a distinctive *argument* about post-pandemic absenteeism rather than a measurement of it: that districts are treating it as a compliance problem (awareness campaigns, attendance awards, warning letters) when it is a symptom of student disengagement, and that the interventions therefore keep underperforming. Her January 2025 survey work found only 41% of grades 3-12 students say they love school against 71% of parents who believe they do — a gap that reframes "why won't they come" as "what are they coming to." For a spring convening on what districts are actually doing, this is the frame that makes the panel an argument instead of a data dump. She is also the strongest pure stage presence on this list: a trade bestseller, a Senate witness, and a working keynote speaker.

**Recent work.**
- [The disengagement gap: Why student engagement isn't what parents expect](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/the-disengagement-gap/) — January 6, 2025 (with Youssef Shoukry and David Nitkin)
- [Testimony, Senate HELP Committee hearing on the State of K-12 Education](https://www.help.senate.gov/hearings/the-state-of-k-12-education) — September 2025
- [The Disengaged Teen](https://www.brookings.edu/books/the-disengaged-teen/) (with Jenny Anderson) — 2025
- Panelist, [Bringing the kids back: New strategies for addressing chronic absenteeism in school](https://www.brookings.edu/events/bringing-the-kids-back-new-strategies-for-addressing-chronic-absenteeism-in-school/) — Brookings event, January 9, 2025

**Recency, honestly.** Her absenteeism-specific output is *Current*, not *Active*: the disengagement work is roughly 20 months old, and her visible 2026 writing has moved to AI and student agency (e.g. [Enhancing student agency through school AI councils](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/enhancing-student-agency-through-school-ai-councils/), August 18, 2026). She has not published new absenteeism data this year. For a media-comment ask that would drop her; for a spring panel it matters much less, because the framework is the contribution and she is still actively speaking from it.

**Background.** Directs the Center for Universal Education; her Senate testimony argued for community-school models, which is directly on the "what districts are doing" question.

**Access.** Program director, so scheduling routes through Brookings comms — but she is also listed with commercial speakers bureaus, which usually signals a fee expectation and a booking calendar that fills months ahead. For a spring event, approach now, and be explicit early about whether this is an honorarium or a fee engagement.

### 2. Michael Hansen — Senior Fellow, Brown Center on Education Policy

**Why them.** Hansen covers the angle no one else on this list does and most absenteeism panels miss entirely: adults not showing up. His 2025 state-data work found teacher absence rates roughly doubled post-pandemic — as high as 10% of teachers out daily, with a near-500% increase over baseline in Clark County — and he argues this is a distinct problem from student absenteeism, because teacher absence hits advantaged and disadvantaged schools about equally while student absence does not. On a stage where every other panelist is discussing how to get kids back, "your substitute coverage is part of the reason they don't want to be there" is the line that makes the session memorable. He is also the most quantitatively grounded Brookings voice available here.

**Recent work.**
- [State data shows K-12 teacher absences surged post-pandemic](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/state-data-shows-k-12-teacher-absences-surged-post-pandemic/) — 2025 (with Purnima Aggarwal and Rebecca Wagner)
- [Brown Center scholars reflect on how the pandemic reshaped US education](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/brown-center-scholars-reflect-on-how-the-pandemic-reshaped-us-education/) — April 2, 2025; his section on the teacher workforce treats "stubbornly high rates of chronic student absenteeism" as a driver of teacher strain
- [Brown Center scholars reflect on education after 1 year of the Trump administration](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/brown-center-scholars-reflect-on-education-after-1-year-of-the-trump-administration/) — January 20, 2026

**Background.** Holds the Herman and George R. Brown Chair; his earlier teacher-absence work goes back to 2020, so this is a sustained line of research rather than a pandemic-era detour, and he has been quoted in the education trade press through 2026.

**Access.** Resident senior fellow, no institutional-leadership calendar, publishes and comments frequently — the most straightforwardly bookable name here. Brown Center / Governance Studies comms is the route.

### 3. Jon Valant — Director, Brown Center on Education Policy; Herman and George R. Brown Chair in Education Studies

**Why them.** Valant is the right person for the governance half of your question — not "is absenteeism high" but "why can't districts act on it." His June 2026 school-boards research is the most current Brookings work on how district decision-making has actually functioned since COVID, and it found the disruption was highly uneven: purple communities and large districts absorbed the most conflict. That is a direct explanation for why the same attendance playbook produces different results in different places. He also opened Brookings' own January 2025 absenteeism convening, so he knows the field and the other names in it.

**Recent work.**
- [Local control, national conflict: School boards in the COVID-19 and culture war era](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/local-control-national-conflict-school-boards-in-the-covid-19-and-culture-war-era/) — June 5, 2026 (with Ariell Bertrand, Rachel M. Perera, and Nicolas Zerbino)
- [The past, present, and future of America's school boards](https://www.brookings.edu/events/the-past-present-and-future-of-americas-school-boards/) — Brookings event, June 2026
- Opening remarks, [Bringing the kids back](https://www.brookings.edu/events/bringing-the-kids-back-new-strategies-for-addressing-chronic-absenteeism-in-school/) — January 9, 2025

**Caveat to state plainly.** The school-boards report contains no absenteeism or attendance analysis; I checked. Valant is not an absenteeism researcher. He is a district-politics researcher who has convened on absenteeism, and he should be pitched that way — as the person who explains the implementation environment, or as your moderator.

**Background.** PK-12 policy and politics, with a research focus on inequities across schools and the policies that widen or narrow them.

**Access.** Center director. Booking runs through Brookings comms and takes longer than a rank-and-file fellow; the offsetting benefit is that a center director's participation often brings the center's convening apparatus with it.

### 4. Kenneth K. Wong — Nonresident Senior Fellow, Governance Studies, Brown Center on Education Policy

**Why them.** Wong is the district-and-city-hall specialist — he wrote *The Education Mayor*, and he is one of the few people on the Brookings education roster carrying a Municipal Government tag alongside K-12. If the panel wants someone who can talk about attendance interventions that reach outside the school building — housing instability, transit, health services, mayoral and county partnerships — this is the seat. He is a useful counterweight to a panel that would otherwise be entirely school-internal.

**Recent work.**
- [Brown Center scholars reflect on education after 1 year of the Trump administration](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/brown-center-scholars-reflect-on-education-after-1-year-of-the-trump-administration/) — January 20, 2026; his section covers the dismantling of the federal role, which is the backdrop to districts absorbing more of this problem alone
- [Brown Center scholars reflect on how the pandemic reshaped US education](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/brown-center-scholars-reflect-on-how-the-pandemic-reshaped-us-education/) — April 2, 2025

**Verification note.** I found no publication by Wong specifically on chronic absenteeism or attendance — that is a statement about my searches as well as about him. He is *Active* at Brookings and squarely credentialed on district governance; the absenteeism connection is inference from his subject area, not evidence, so confirm his interest in a pre-call before committing him to the topic.

**Background.** Professor at Brown University; Brookings is his secondary affiliation. Long-running work on urban district governance and mayoral control.

**Access.** Nonresident, so his day job is at Brown — expect a slower reply and a second press office. Academic calendar means spring dates are workable but need lead time.

## Why this order

The ranking is driven by a scarcity, not by a ladder of prestige. Only Winthrop and Hansen have their own current work bearing on absenteeism, and they cover opposite halves of it — why students disengage, and why the adults are also missing — so a two-person version of this panel is coherent on its own and is what I would book if you can only take two. Valant and Wong rank below them because their fit is inferential: strong on the district-capacity question your framing raises, silent on absenteeism itself in everything I could verify. Going down the list trades subject-matter authority for governance range. The alternative tradeoff worth naming: Winthrop is the marquee draw but her absenteeism material dates to early 2025, while Hansen has the fresher numbers and much less stage wattage. For a convening that wants an argument, take Winthrop; for one that wants findings, lead with Hansen.

## Gaps

**No Brookings scholar currently owns the empirical US absenteeism beat.** The strongest absenteeism research hosted on brookings.edu is by non-Brookings authors: the UNC EPIC team (Swiderski, Fuller, Bastian, [September 9, 2024](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/student-level-attendance-patterns-show-depth-breadth-and-persistence-of-post-pandemic-absenteeism/)) and a USC survey team (Saavedra, Polikoff, Silver, [March 26, 2024](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/parents-are-not-fully-aware-of-or-concerned-about-their-childrens-school-attendance/)). A [March 23, 2026 Brookings piece](https://www.brookings.edu/articles/survey-shows-alarming-drop-in-working-conditions-for-teachers-what-are-we-doing-about-it/) touching absenteeism is likewise by outside authors (Baker and Koedel, University of Missouri). If you want a Brookings byline on stage you are choosing from this memo; if you want the person with the freshest national numbers, they are not at Brookings, and Brookings itself reached outside for its own January 2025 panel — to AEI's Nat Malkus, Baltimore City Schools CEO Sonja Brookins Santelises, and Transcend's Jenee Henry Wood. Those three, plus Attendance Works and the Everyone Graduates Center, are the natural non-Brookings complements. Worth noting the guest authors above are themselves bookable and have already published with Brookings.

**No practitioner voice.** Nobody in this list runs a district. The "what districts are actually doing" half of your framing will be answered from research, not from operating experience, unless you pair a Brookings scholar with a superintendent — which is precisely what Brookings did on this exact topic in 2025.

**Uncovered angles.** Nobody here works on absence driven by disability, chronic illness, or long COVID; on immigration-enforcement fear as an attendance suppressant, which is live in 2026; or on the tribal, rural, and small-district picture, where the recovery pattern differs from the large urban districts most of this research covers.

**Verification limits.** Recency here comes from web search, not from a bundled activity feed — Brookings profile pages render client-side and list nothing. Where I write "no absenteeism work found" for Wong, Harris, or Perera, that is the result of two or more query formulations, not a claim that none exists. The roster index behind this memo was built 2026-09-13; anyone who joined Brookings very recently would not appear in it.
