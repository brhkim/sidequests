# Brookings experts for a spring panel on chronic absenteeism

**Prepared:** September 2026
**Ask:** one or two Brookings experts who would be good on stage on chronic absenteeism and what districts are doing about it post-pandemic.

## Bottom line

Two names I'd lead with, for different reasons:

1. **Rebecca Winthrop** — the best *stage* pick. Trade-book author, ran Brookings' own public event on exactly this topic, frames absenteeism as student disengagement rather than a compliance problem.
2. **Michael Hansen** — the best *data* pick, and the one with genuinely recent Brookings research in this space (May 2025). Brings the supply-side angle: teachers are missing school too, which is a district-operations story most absenteeism panels skip.

One important caveat up front: Brookings' visible output on *student* chronic absenteeism is thinner than a web search suggests, because several of the most-cited "Brookings" absenteeism pieces were written by outside academics publishing on the Brown Center Chalkboard blog, and one frequently-named Brookings absenteeism economist has since left the institution. Details in the "Do not book as Brookings" section — worth reading before anyone drafts a program.

---

## Recommended

### Rebecca Winthrop
Director, Center for Universal Education; Senior Fellow, Global Economy and Development. Current.

- Co-author of *The Disengaged Teen: Helping Kids Learn Better, Feel Better, and Live Better* (with journalist Jenny Anderson, January 2025) — a mainstream trade book on exactly the "why aren't kids showing up / showing up but checked out" question.
- Co-author of the CUE/Transcend report *The Disengagement Gap: Why Student Engagement Isn't What Parents Expect* (January 2025). Headline finding that plays well from a stage: only 41% of 3rd–12th graders say they love school, but 71% of parents believe their child does.
- Hosted and spoke at the Brookings event **"Bringing the kids back: New strategies for addressing chronic absenteeism in school"** (Falk Auditorium, January 9, 2025), co-hosted by CUE and the Brown Center. Fellow panelists were Sonja Brookins Santelises (CEO, Baltimore City Schools) and Jenee Henry Wood (Transcend) — which tells you she is comfortable sharing a stage with practitioners, which is the format you're describing.

*Why she fits:* she is the closest thing Brookings has to a public voice on this, she reframes absenteeism as engagement (which gives a panel somewhere to go beyond "rates are still bad"), and she has an existing working relationship with a big-city superintendent if you want a district practitioner alongside her.

*Caveat:* her institutional home is the *global* education center; her book and the disengagement report are US-facing, but she is not primarily a US district-policy analyst. Pair her with someone who has US administrative data.

### Michael Hansen
Senior Fellow, Brown Center on Education Policy; co-editor of the Brown Center Chalkboard. Current.

- Labor economist; core areas are teacher policy, teacher quality and school accountability.
- **"State data shows K-12 teacher absences surged post-pandemic"** (Brookings, May 19, 2025, with Purnima Aggarwal and Rebecca Wagner) — analysis of teacher absence data from Connecticut, Illinois, Nevada and South Carolina. Findings: post-pandemic teacher absence rates exceed pre-pandemic baselines in all four states; Clark County, NV (Las Vegas) spiked nearly 500% over its 2019 baseline on a mix of quarantine policy and 2023 sickouts, versus roughly 6–7 percentage-point rises in NYC and Chicago; and unlike *student* absenteeism, teacher absence rose about equally in high- and low-poverty schools.
- Longer track record on the same theme: "We should be focusing on absenteeism among teachers, not just students" (Jan 2020, with Diana Quintero) and "Focusing on organizational culture—not just policies—can reduce teacher absenteeism" (Apr 2020).

*Why he fits:* this is the freshest Brookings-authored absenteeism research, and it gives the panel a district-operations argument with teeth — substitute coverage, staffing, and the fact that adult attendance and student attendance are entangled. Good foil to Winthrop's engagement framing.

## Also worth considering

### Lauren Bauer
Fellow, Economic Studies; Associate Director, The Hamilton Project. Current.

The accountability-policy voice. Co-author of the Hamilton Project strategy paper **"Reducing Chronic Absenteeism under the Every Student Succeeds Act"** (with Patrick Liu, Diane Whitmore Schanzenbach and Jay Shambaugh) and of **"First Look: Where chronic student absence is a problem and strategies to make progress"** (Aug 2018, with Becca Portman), plus the Hamilton Project's interactive chronic-absence maps. That work is directly responsible for a policy fact your panel will want: 36 states, DC and Puerto Rico chose to hold schools accountable for chronic absenteeism in their ESSA plans.

*Caveat, and it matters for a "post-pandemic" panel:* her absenteeism work is substantially 2016–2018 and pre-COVID. Book her if you want the accountability-design thread (does ESSA chronic-absence accountability still work when a third of a district is chronically absent?), not for current district practice.

### Jon Valant
Director, Brown Center on Education Policy; Senior Fellow, Governance Studies. Current.

Not an absenteeism researcher, but he covers K-12 policy and politics, school choice, discipline and educational inequity, and speaks regularly in the press on unequal pandemic recovery. Best used as a **moderator** or as the person who situates absenteeism inside the broader recovery-and-accountability fight, rather than as a content expert on attendance.

## Do not book as Brookings

- **Sofoklis Goulas** — comes up constantly in searches as a Brookings education economist. His Hamilton Project fellowship ran **2023–2025**; as of 2026 he is a Senior Researcher at Foundry10, a Research Affiliate at Yale's MacMillan Center, and co-founder/director of public-policy programs at the Aletheia Research Institution. Still a good speaker on this material — just not a Brookings affiliation, so don't put "Brookings" next to his name on the program.
- **Tom Swiderski, Sarah Crittenden Fuller, Kevin C. Bastian** — authors of the widely cited "Student-level attendance patterns show depth, breadth, and persistence of post-pandemic absenteeism" (Brookings, Sept 9, 2024). Excellent work (17% of students chronically absent at least once in a three-year pre-pandemic window vs. 40% post-pandemic; the 95th-percentile student went from 61 to 111 absent days over three years). But they are **UNC-Chapel Hill / EPIC** researchers who published on the Brown Center Chalkboard. Invite them on their own merits, not as Brookings scholars.
- **Brian A. Jacob and Kelly Lovett** ("Chronic absenteeism: An old problem in search of new answers," 2017) — University of Michigan, not Brookings staff, and the piece predates the pandemic.

## If you want a non-Brookings second chair

The most active current trackers of post-pandemic district absenteeism are outside Brookings, and a panel of two Brookings scholars plus one of these would be stronger than three Brookings names:

- **Nat Malkus (AEI)** — the running "Return to Learn"-style absenteeism tracker; reports through 2024 and 2025 ("Lingering Absence in Public Schools," "From Attendance Crisis to Chronic Condition?").
- **RAND's American School District Panel team** — *Chronic Absenteeism Still a Struggle in 2024–2025* and *Districts Try with Limited Success to Reduce Chronic Absenteeism*, which is precisely the "what districts are actually doing" evidence base, including the uncomfortable finding that most district strategies have not moved rates much.
- **Hedy Chang, Attendance Works** — the practitioner-facing organization most districts actually use; good counterweight to a room full of researchers.

## Suggested framing for the ask

When you reach out, the panel question that makes best use of these two is roughly: *rates came down from the 2022 peak but are still far above baseline — is that a student-motivation problem, a school-capacity problem, or an accountability-design problem?* Winthrop argues the first, Hansen has data for the second, Bauer wrote the framework for the third. That is a real disagreement, which is what makes a panel worth attending.
